# EP002 — Pearl Harbor: The Warnings Washington Ignored

Channel: Brinkman · Format: Countdown (geopolitics) · Target: about 18 minutes (about 2,700 spoken words at Kokoro pace)
Status: v1, 2026-11-22. Publish by about 3 December 2026, ahead of the 85th anniversary (7 December). Times are local Hawaii time unless Washington is named. Casualty figures follow the National Park Service and the US Navy; sources at the end.

---

[S01] HOOK
Seven o'clock on a Sunday morning. On a hilltop on the north shore of Oahu, two young Army privates are watching a radar screen that is supposed to be switched off. Then the biggest blip either of them has ever seen appears. Something enormous, a hundred and thirty-seven miles to the north, and coming closer. They phone it in. The officer on duty listens, and says five words: don't worry about it. Fifty minutes later, the first bombs fall on Pearl Harbor. That blip was not the first warning. It was the last of dozens, spread over almost a year, read by the most powerful men in Washington, and every one of them was missed.

[S02] INTRO
Good evening, this is Brinkman. Every war starts at the brink, and tonight's brink is a countdown. Twelve days in late 1941, when the United States could read Japan's secret messages, sent its commanders a war warning, and still got caught asleep. This is not a story about one mistake. It is a story about signals, buried in noise, passed between people who never put them together. So let's open the file, and start the clock.

[S03] THE OIL NOOSE
To understand the attack, start with oil. By 1941 Japan had been at war in China for four years, and it imported about eighty percent of its oil from the United States. In July 1941, Japanese troops marched into southern French Indochina, putting bombers within reach of British Malaya and the Dutch East Indies. Washington answered by freezing Japanese assets. Britain and the Dutch followed, and by August, American oil had stopped flowing to Japan. Tokyo's navy had perhaps two years of fuel in its tanks. Its leaders saw only two ways out: give up the war in China, which the army refused to do, or seize the oil fields of the Dutch East Indies by force. And between Japan and that oil sat the American fleet, which President Roosevelt had moved from California to Pearl Harbor in 1940, as a warning.

[S04] YAMAMOTO'S GAMBLE
The man who planned the answer was Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto, commander of Japan's Combined Fleet. He had studied at Harvard and served in Washington. He had seen American factories and oil fields, and he believed Japan could not win a long war against them. So he proposed a gamble: destroy the US Pacific Fleet at its anchorage in one blow, and buy six months, maybe a year, to take Southeast Asia. In November 1940 the British had shown it could be done, when old biplanes from one carrier crippled three Italian battleships in the harbor of Taranto. Pearl Harbor was only about forty feet deep, too shallow, the US Navy believed, for torpedoes dropped from the air. Japanese engineers fixed that with wooden fins. Pilots practiced low runs over a bay in southern Japan, again and again.

[S05] MAGIC
And here is the twist. While Japan prepared, the United States was reading its mail. In 1940 a team of Army codebreakers led by William Friedman and Frank Rowlett cracked the machine cipher that Japan's Foreign Ministry used for its most secret diplomatic cables. The Americans called it Purple. They built copies of the Japanese machine without ever seeing one, and the intelligence it produced was given a codename: Magic. But Magic had limits. It read diplomats, not admirals. The navy's operational code was only partly broken, and almost nothing about the fleet's plans went out by diplomatic cable. Magic went to fewer than a dozen people in Washington. Purple machines went to the Philippines and to Britain. The commanders in Hawaii never got one.

[S06] THE EARLY WARNINGS
The first warnings came early. In January 1941, the American ambassador in Tokyo, Joseph Grew, cabled Washington that the Peruvian minister had heard a rumor: in case of trouble with the United States, Japan planned a surprise attack on Pearl Harbor. Naval intelligence judged it not credible. Then, in September, Tokyo sent its consulate in Honolulu an unusual request. Divide the waters of Pearl Harbor into five areas, and report exactly which warships are moored in each one. The consulate's spy, a young naval officer working under a false name, Takeo Yoshikawa, began sending regular reports. Washington decoded the request in October. Some officers called it a bomb plot. Most saw Japanese thoroughness, the same kind of reports Japan collected on ports from Panama to Manila. It was never sent to Hawaii.

[S07] THE DEADLINE
By November, the talks in Washington between Secretary of State Cordell Hull and the Japanese ambassador, Kichisaburo Nomura, had stalled. And thanks to Magic, Hull knew something Nomura could not say out loud. Tokyo had given its diplomats a deadline. First the 25th of November. Then, in a message decoded on the 22nd, a final extension to the 29th. After that, Tokyo wrote, things are automatically going to happen. Washington now knew that Japan had set a clock. It did not know what would happen when it ran out, or where. On the 25th of November, Roosevelt warned his war cabinet that the United States was likely to be attacked, perhaps as soon as the following Monday. Every eye in the room was on Southeast Asia.

[S08] NOVEMBER 26
Twelve days left. On the 26th of November, Hull handed the Japanese a ten-point note. It demanded that Japan withdraw its forces from China and Indochina, terms Tokyo had already said it could never accept. That same day, in the foggy Kuril Islands far to the north of Japan, a fleet of six aircraft carriers weighed anchor. Two battleships, cruisers, destroyers and tankers went with them, more than four hundred aircraft in all. They took the empty northern route across the Pacific, where merchant ships rarely sailed, in rough winter seas, under total radio silence. Meanwhile, American scouts spotted a different Japanese convoy, heading south off Formosa, packed with troops. That was the fleet everyone could see. So that was the fleet everyone watched.

[S09] NOVEMBER 27: WAR WARNING
Eleven days left. On the 27th of November, the Navy in Washington sent the strongest message in its history to Admiral Husband Kimmel, commander of the Pacific Fleet in Hawaii. It began: this dispatch is to be considered a war warning. An aggressive move by Japan was expected within the next few days. It listed the likely targets: the Philippines, Thailand, the Kra Peninsula, or possibly Borneo. Hawaii was not on the list. The Army sent its own message to Lieutenant General Walter Short, who commanded the defense of the islands. Hostile action was possible at any moment. If war came, Japan should commit the first overt act. And take care not to alarm the civilian population.

[S10] THE SABOTAGE ALERT
General Short read that message and saw his biggest danger inside Hawaii, not out at sea. About a third of the islands' people were of Japanese descent, and Short feared sabotage. So he chose the lowest of his three alert levels, the sabotage alert. Aircraft were pulled out of their dispersal areas and parked wingtip to wingtip in neat rows, where guards could watch them. Ammunition was locked away, to keep it safe from saboteurs. Then Short cabled Washington: department alerted to prevent sabotage, liaison with Navy. In Washington, the reply was filed. Nobody wrote back to ask: sabotage, against what? The planes stayed in their rows for ten days, perfectly placed for an attack from the air.

[S11] THE CARRIERS SAIL
Meanwhile, Admiral Kimmel used the war warning to reinforce the outer islands, where he expected trouble. On the 28th of November, the carrier Enterprise sailed to deliver fighter planes to Wake Island. On the 5th of December, the Lexington left to carry bombers to Midway. The third Pacific carrier, the Saratoga, was in California. The battleships, the pride of the fleet, stayed in harbor, tied up in pairs along Battleship Row, as they did on almost every weekend. No one ordered the carriers out to save them. Kimmel simply thought a carrier was more useful at sea. It was a lucky decision, and it would change the war.

[S12] MID QUESTION
Quick question before the clock runs on. If you had been Admiral Kimmel on the 27th of November, holding a war warning that named every target except Hawaii, what would you have done differently? Tell me in the comments. I'll read the best answers in the next episode.

[S13] THE CARRIERS VANISH
Six days left. On the 1st of December, Japan's navy changed all its radio call signs, for the second time in a month. American radio intelligence in Hawaii, which tracked Japanese ships by their radio traffic, suddenly lost the aircraft carriers. On the 2nd, Kimmel's intelligence officer, Lieutenant Commander Edwin Layton, admitted he did not know where two of the carrier divisions were. Kimmel's reply became famous: do you mean to say they could be rounding Diamond Head, and you wouldn't know it? Layton said he hoped they would be sighted before then. That same day, the fleet at sea received a coded order from Tokyo: climb Mount Niitaka, one two zero eight. It meant: attack on the 8th of December, Japanese time. The 7th, in Hawaii.

[S14] BURN THE CODES
Five days left. On the 1st and 2nd of December, Tokyo ordered its embassies in Washington, London, Hong Kong, Singapore and Manila to destroy their code books and their code machines. Magic read every word. Destroying your codes is what an embassy does when it expects war, and soon. Admiral Kimmel was told on the 3rd. It did not change his alert. On the 2nd, Tokyo asked the Honolulu consulate for something new: daily reports on the warships in Pearl Harbor, and whether the base was protected by barrage balloons or torpedo nets. Barrage balloons and torpedo nets only matter for one thing: an attack from the air. That request was intercepted too. Washington did not translate it until after the attack.

[S15] FLOWERS IN BLOOM
Two days left. On the 5th of December, the FBI recorded a strange phone call from a newspaper in Tokyo to a Japanese dentist in Honolulu. The caller asked about the weather, the searchlights, the aircraft overhead, and which flowers were in bloom: hibiscus, poinsettias. Army intelligence in Hawaii studied the transcript on the evening of the 6th, and could not decide whether it was a code or just small talk. They agreed to look at it again in the morning. That same evening, the spy Yoshikawa sent his last report from the consulate. No barrage balloons. No torpedo nets. In his words, there was a considerable opportunity left for a surprise attack. It reached Washington's codebreakers. They decoded it on the 8th.

[S16] THIS MEANS WAR
The last night. On the 6th of December, Tokyo began sending Ambassador Nomura a long reply to the Hull note, in fourteen parts. By evening, thirteen parts had been decoded. At about half past nine in Washington, a young naval officer carried them to the White House in a locked pouch. Roosevelt read them in his study, then turned to his adviser Harry Hopkins and said: this means war. But the message named no target, and no time. The Navy's top officer, Admiral Harold Stark, was at the theater that evening, and no new warning went out that night. The Army's chief of staff, General George Marshall, was not reached at all. Nobody phoned Hawaii.

[S17] THE ONE O'CLOCK MESSAGE
The morning of the 7th. The fourteenth part arrived: Japan was breaking off negotiations. Then a short extra message, and this one was different. Ambassador Nomura was to deliver the full reply at exactly one o'clock in the afternoon, Washington time. An Army intelligence officer, Colonel Rufus Bratton, saw it at once. Why one o'clock, on a Sunday? One o'clock in Washington was half past seven in the morning in Hawaii. But General Marshall was out on his Sunday horseback ride, and did not reach his office until after eleven. He read all fourteen parts, then wrote a warning by hand. He chose not to use the telephone, which he feared was not secure. The Army's radio link to Hawaii was down because of static. So the most urgent warning of the war went out by commercial telegram, with no priority marking.

[S18] THE SUBMARINE
In Hawaii, the warnings had already started arriving on their own. At 3:42 in the morning, a minesweeper spotted a periscope near the harbor entrance. At 6:45, the destroyer USS Ward found a small submarine trailing a cargo ship into the harbor, opened fire, hit its conning tower, and dropped depth charges on it. These were the first American shots of the Pacific War, more than an hour before the attack. The Ward radioed its report. But false submarine sightings were common around Oahu, and the report moved slowly up the chain of command, from one officer to another, each one asking for confirmation.

[S19] THE BLIP
At two minutes past seven, at the Opana radar station on Oahu's north shore, Privates Joseph Lockard and George Elliott were keeping their new radar running past its seven o'clock shutdown, just for practice. Then the blip appeared, 137 miles to the north. They called the information center at Fort Shafter. The only officer there was Lieutenant Kermit Tyler, a fighter pilot on only his second shift in the room, with no real training for the job. Tyler knew that a flight of American B-17 bombers was due in from California that morning. Hawaii's radio stations played music all night to guide them in. It was the same music the Japanese navigators tuned in to find Oahu. Tyler told them: don't worry about it.

[S20] 7:55 A.M.
At 7:48, the first wave of Japanese aircraft reached Oahu, and minutes later the bombs began to fall. Over two waves, three hundred and fifty-three planes struck airfields and Battleship Row. Torpedoes, with their wooden fins, ran perfectly through the shallow water. A bomb set off the forward magazine of the USS Arizona, and 1,177 of her crew were killed. In under two hours, nineteen ships were sunk or damaged, including all eight battleships, and about one hundred and eighty-eight aircraft were destroyed, most of them parked wingtip to wingtip. 2,403 Americans were killed, sixty-eight of them civilians. Japan lost twenty-nine aircraft, five midget submarines, and sixty-four men. In Washington, Japan's diplomats delivered their message at twenty past two, an hour after the bombs had fallen. And General Marshall's telegram? It reached General Short's headquarters hours after the attack. It was carried from the telegraph office by a young motorcycle messenger, himself of Japanese descent.

[S21] WHO WAS TO BLAME
Within weeks, a presidential commission blamed the two commanders in Hawaii for dereliction of duty. Kimmel and Short were relieved and retired at lower ranks, and spent the rest of their lives fighting to clear their names. Over the following years, there were nine official investigations. The final one, by Congress in 1946, spread the blame much wider: to Washington, which had read the warnings and failed to pass them on clearly. Some writers have claimed Roosevelt knew the target and let the attack happen. Historians have searched for decades, and found no evidence of that. The truth is less dramatic, and more troubling. In 1962 the historian Roberta Wohlstetter described it as a problem of signals and noise. Every warning existed. But they sat in different offices, under different secrets, surrounded by thousands of false alarms, and no single person ever held them all at once. That failure is why, in 1947, the United States created a Central Intelligence Agency, and a single system to put the pieces together.

[S22] OUTRO
So that's Pearl Harbor. An embargo that put Japan on a clock, codebreakers who could read the clock ticking, a war warning that named every target but one, and a Sunday morning when the last signals were waved away. Japan won the morning, and lost the war it started. The carriers it missed were at sea, and six months later, at Midway, American carriers sank four of the six that attacked Pearl Harbor. Eighty-five years on, the question is still the one that haunts every intelligence service on Earth: what are we missing right now? Next time on Brinkman: another war, and the brink it started from.
