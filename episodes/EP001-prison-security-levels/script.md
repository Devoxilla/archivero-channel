# EP001 — Every Prison Security Level Explained

Channel: The Archivero · Narrator: the Archivero · Target length: about 11 minutes (about 1,950 words at Kokoro's pace)
Status: v2 for review, 2026-11-19 (lengthened from v1; fact checks resolved in the Sources section)

---

[S01] HOOK
There is a prison in Colorado where the window is about four inches wide. It is built so you can see the sky, but not where you are. Most people in the American federal system will never get anywhere near it. To reach it, you have to fall through every other level first. Today we climb down the whole ladder, one rung at a time, all the way to that window.

[S02] INTRO
Good evening. I'm the Archivero. I keep the files nobody asked for, in a room nobody visits, by the light of a lamp that never quite goes out. Tonight's file is a thick one: every security level of the federal prison system in the United States. Roughly a hundred and fifty-five thousand people live somewhere on this ladder right now. Some of them can walk out the front door every morning. Some of them have not seen another prisoner's face in years. The difference between those two lives is not luck. It is a number. So before we open the first door, let's look at how that number is made.

[S03] HOW YOU GET YOUR LEVEL
When someone is sentenced to federal prison, the Bureau of Prisons does not simply pick a building with a free bed. It fills in a scoring form. Points for how serious the crime was. Points for any history of violence. Points for any past escape, even an old one. Points for how long is left on the sentence, and for any other charges waiting in the wings. Add them up, and you get a security level. Then staff can adjust it with what they call management variables: a gang conflict, a medical need, a request to be closer to family. The score is not fixed, either. Behave for years, and the points drift down. Start fights, and they climb. In other words, the ladder moves in both directions, and every rung is earned.

[S04] LEVEL 0 — THE HALFWAY HOUSE
We start at the bottom of the ladder, which is barely a ladder. A Residential Reentry Center, better known as a halfway house. The Bureau of Prisons contracts them to help people near the end of a sentence rebuild a normal life. Residents go out to work, look for housing, reconnect with family, then come back at night and sign in. No cells. No bars. Just a curfew, drug tests, and a staff member who knows exactly what time you said you would be back. Some people finish the very last stretch at home instead, on home confinement, with phone check-ins and the occasional surprise visit. It is the only kind of prison where the hardest part is the paperwork. Break the rules, though, and the ladder works in reverse. You go back up.

[S05] LEVEL 1 — MINIMUM SECURITY: THE CAMP
Next, the federal prison camp. Minimum security. Dormitory housing, a relatively low number of staff, and limited or no perimeter fencing. Yes, at some of them you could technically walk away. People mostly don't, because walking away is a new federal crime, it adds years, and it sends you several rungs up. Camps are built around work: kitchens, groundskeeping, maintenance, sometimes jobs at the bigger prison next door. Many camps sit right beside a higher-security institution, like a small cottage in the shadow of a castle. The press likes to call them "Club Fed." When Martha Stewart served her sentence at the camp in Alderson, West Virginia, tabloids nicknamed it "Camp Cupcake." It is not a resort. There are counts, searches, and rules for everything. It is just the level where the walls are mostly in your head.

[S06] LEVEL 2 — LOW SECURITY
One step up: low security. Here the fence appears, and it brings a friend. Low-security federal institutions have double-fenced perimeters. Most people still live in dormitories or cubicles, rows of bunks separated by half walls, so privacy is a rumour. There are more officers per inmate than at a camp, and the day is organised around counts, work assignments, and education programs. Many people here were convicted of non-violent crimes but have sentences too long, or histories too complicated, for a camp. Think of it as the first level where the building is actively watching you back.

[S07] PATTERN INTERRUPT
Quick question before we go deeper. Which level do you think holds the most people? Leave your guess in the comments. I'll give you the answer at the end of the file, and it is less dramatic than you'd hope, which is exactly why it matters.

[S08] LEVEL 3 — MEDIUM SECURITY
Medium security. The perimeter gets stronger, often double fences with electronic detection systems, so touching the wire is not a secret for long. Housing changes too: mostly cells now, not dorms. That means a door, a bunk, a toilet, and a roommate you did not choose. Medium institutions run a wide range of work and treatment programs, from factory jobs to drug treatment to classes, but movement is more controlled. You go where you are told, when you are told, and the schedule is the real warden. Staff numbers rise again, and so do internal controls: more searches, more cameras, more counts, including in the middle of the night. This is the middle of the ladder, and for many people it is the place where a long sentence actually happens, year after identical year.

[S09] A DAY ON THE LADDER
It helps to picture one ordinary day. Wake-up comes early, then a count, where everyone must be exactly where they are supposed to be. Breakfast. Work call: the kitchen, the laundry, the factory floor, the classroom. Another count. Lunch. More work. Recreation, if the yard is open. Mail call. Dinner. Another count. Lockdown. And then, while you sleep, more counts, a flashlight checking that the shape in the bed is still breathing. Change the level, and the shape of the day stays almost the same. What changes is the space around it: how far you can walk, how many doors you pass, and how many people are watching while you do.

[S10] LEVEL 4 — HIGH SECURITY: THE PENITENTIARY
High security. In the federal system these are the United States Penitentiaries, the USPs. Highly secured perimeters, with walls or reinforced fences, and towers or patrols watching them. Single and multiple-occupant cells. The highest ratio of staff to inmates of the regular levels, and close control of every movement: when you eat, when you walk, when you shower. This is where the system sends people convicted of the most violent crimes, or with histories of escape, gang activity, or violence inside prison. The most famous penitentiary of all is closed. Alcatraz operated as a federal prison from 1934 to 1963, on a rock in the cold water of San Francisco Bay. Over those years, thirty-six men tried to escape it in fourteen attempts. Most were caught. Some were shot. Two drowned. Officially, none made it. But five were never found, including the three men who left behind papier-mâché heads in their beds one night in June 1962 and paddled into the dark on a raft made of raincoats.

[S11] THE PRISON INSIDE THE PRISON
But there is a level inside every level. Most federal institutions have a Special Housing Unit, the SHU, or as inmates call it, "the hole." This is where people go for breaking rules, during investigations, or for their own protection. Time in the SHU usually means being locked in a cell for most of the day, with meals delivered through a slot and recreation in a small cage. It is not a separate prison you are sentenced to. It is a place the prison can send you, any day, from any level. Which is why, in practice, every security level has a basement.

[S12] LEVEL 5 — ADMINISTRATIVE AND THE SUPERMAX
And now, the bottom of the file. The Bureau of Prisons calls the last category "administrative." These institutions have special missions: holding people before trial, treating serious medical cases, or containing people considered extremely dangerous, violent, or escape-prone. The most extreme is ADX Florence in Colorado, opened in 1994 and nicknamed "the Alcatraz of the Rockies." Cells are roughly seven by twelve feet, with a concrete bed, a concrete desk, a concrete stool, and a shower. Most prisoners there spend about twenty-three hours a day alone in the cell. Food comes through a slot. Exercise happens alone, in an outdoor pen that some former prisoners have compared to an empty swimming pool. And the window is about four inches wide, angled so you see sky but not the landscape, so you cannot tell where in the building you are. Over the years, the people held there have included Zacarias Moussaoui, Dzhokhar Tsarnaev, and Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán. There is a step-down program, a slow climb through several phases that can, after years of good conduct, lead back to an ordinary penitentiary. At this level, the punishment is not the walls. It is the silence between them.

[S13] OUTRO
So, the answer to the question. The level holding the most people is not the supermax and not the penitentiary. It is low security. In recent Bureau of Prisons figures, it holds more people than any other level, well over a third of everyone. The ladder is not a tower of monsters. It is mostly a long, crowded middle. And here is the strangest detail in the whole file. In Florence, Colorado, a single federal complex holds a camp, a medium, a penitentiary, and the supermax, side by side on the same stretch of road. The whole ladder, from walk-away fences to a four-inch window, within sight of one another. Next time, we open the file on the strangest punishments in history. I'll leave the lamp on.
