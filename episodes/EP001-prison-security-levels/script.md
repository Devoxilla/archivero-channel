# EP001 — Every Prison Security Level Explained

Channel: The Archivero · Narrator: the Archivero · Format: animated doodle story · Target: about 10 minutes
Status: v3 (story version), 2026-11-19. Gary is fictional; every rule he runs into is real (see Sources).

---

[S01] HOOK
There is a prison in Colorado where the window is about four inches wide. It is built so you can see the sky, but not where you are. Most people in the American prison system never get anywhere near it. Gary did. And Gary started out as the least dangerous man you have ever met. This is the story of how he fell through every single level to get there.

[S02] INTRO
Good evening. I'm the Archivero. I keep the files nobody asked for. Tonight's file is about a man named Gary. Gary isn't real, but every rule he runs into is. Roughly a hundred and fifty-five thousand people live somewhere on the federal prison ladder right now. Some of them walk out the front door every morning to go to work. Some of them have not touched another human being in years. The difference between those two lives is not luck. It is a number. So let's watch Gary get his.

[S03] HOW YOU GET YOUR LEVEL
Gary is thirty-four, an accountant, and he has just been convicted of fraud. He moved money that was not his. No weapons, no violence, no record. When someone is sentenced to federal prison, the Bureau of Prisons does not just pick a building with a free bed. It fills in a scoring form. Points for how serious the crime was. Points for any history of violence. Points for any past escape. Points for how long is left on the sentence, and for other charges still waiting. Gary's total is tiny. Staff can still adjust the number for things like gang conflicts or medical needs, and it keeps moving for the rest of the sentence. Behave, and it drifts down. Cause trouble, and it climbs. Gary's number says: minimum security. Gary is relieved. Gary should enjoy this moment.

[S04] LEVEL 0 — THE HALFWAY HOUSE
Before we follow Gary in, look at where he could end up if everything goes right. The very bottom of the ladder is the halfway house, officially a Residential Reentry Center. Near the end of a sentence, people move there to rebuild a normal life. They go out to work, look for an apartment, see their families, and come back at night to sign in. No cells. No bars. Just a curfew, drug tests, and a staff member who knows exactly what time you said you would be back. Some people even finish the last stretch at home, on home confinement, with phone check-ins and surprise visits. That is Gary's finish line. All he has to do is keep his head down for a few years.

[S05] LEVEL 1 — MINIMUM SECURITY: THE CAMP
Gary arrives at a federal prison camp. Minimum security. Dormitories instead of cells, very few staff, and limited or no fencing at all. The press likes to call these places "Club Fed." When Martha Stewart served her sentence at the camp in Alderson, West Virginia, the tabloids nicknamed it "Camp Cupcake." Gary gets a job in the kitchen. There are counts and rules for everything, but the field outside is wide open. And one night, after a bad phone call from home, Gary does the one thing you must never do at a camp. He walks away. Here is the catch nobody tells you. Walking away from a camp is called escape. It is a brand new federal crime, and it adds years. Gary makes it as far as a gas station. By the next afternoon he is back in handcuffs, and his little number is not so little anymore.

[S06] LEVEL 2 — LOW SECURITY
Gary's new score sends him one step up: low security. Here the fence appears, and it brings a friend. Low-security prisons have double fences around the whole perimeter. Gary still sleeps in a dormitory, in a cubicle of bunks separated by half walls, so privacy is a rumour. There are more officers than at the camp, and the whole day runs on counts, work assignments, and classes. Most of the people around Gary were convicted of non-violent crimes, just with longer sentences. Gary tells himself this is still fine. Then another man takes his seat in the TV room. Gary, who has never hit anyone in his life, decides tonight is the night to start.

[S07] PATTERN INTERRUPT
Quick question before Gary goes any deeper. Which level do you think holds the most people? Leave your guess in the comments. I'll give you the answer at the end of the file, and it is less dramatic than you'd hope.

[S08] LEVEL 3 — MEDIUM SECURITY
A fight inside prison is a gift to your security score, in the worst possible way. Gary moves up to medium security. The perimeter gets stronger, often double fences with electronic detection systems, so touching the wire is not a secret for long. And the dorm is gone. Now Gary lives in a cell: a door, a bunk, a toilet, and a roommate he did not choose. Medium prisons run all kinds of jobs and programs, from factory work to drug treatment, but movement is controlled. You go where you are told, when you are told. For a lot of people this middle level is where a long sentence actually happens, year after identical year. Gary has just added several of them.

[S09] A DAY ON THE LADDER
Here is one of Gary's days. Wake-up comes early, then a count, where every single person must be exactly where they are supposed to be. Breakfast. Work call: the kitchen, the laundry, the factory floor. Another count. Lunch. More work. The yard, if it is open. Mail call, which for Gary is mostly silence. Dinner. Another count. Lockdown. And then, while he sleeps, more counts, a flashlight beam checking that the shape in the bed is still breathing. Change the level and the shape of the day barely changes. What changes is how far you can walk, how many doors you pass, and how many people are watching you do it.

[S10] LEVEL 4 — HIGH SECURITY: THE PENITENTIARY
Gary joins the wrong group in the yard for protection. Then a fight with an officer. Now he is sent to high security: a United States Penitentiary. Walls or reinforced fences, towers and patrols, cells for one or two people, and the most staff per prisoner of any regular level. Every movement is controlled: when you eat, when you walk, when you shower. Gary's new cellmate loves one story above all, the most famous penitentiary in history. Alcatraz, a federal prison on a rock in San Francisco Bay from 1934 to 1963. Thirty-six men tried to escape it in fourteen attempts. Officially, none made it. But five were never found, including three men who, one night in June 1962, left papier-mâché heads in their beds and paddled into the dark on a raft made of raincoats. Gary listens, and starts measuring the air vent.

[S11] THE PRISON INSIDE THE PRISON
He does not get far. Gary is caught with tools and goes to the Special Housing Unit, the SHU, which inmates call "the hole." Almost every federal prison has one. It is where people go for breaking rules, during investigations, or for their own protection. Time in the SHU means a cell for most of the day, meals pushed through a slot in the door, and recreation in something that looks a lot like a cage. You are not sentenced to the SHU. The prison can send you there any day, from any level. Which is why, in practice, every security level has a basement.

[S12] LEVEL 5 — ADMINISTRATIVE: THE SUPERMAX
In the hole, Gary loses his temper one last time and seriously injures an officer. And that is how a quiet accountant reaches the bottom of the file: ADX Florence, in Colorado, the federal supermax, opened in 1994 and nicknamed "the Alcatraz of the Rockies." The Bureau of Prisons calls this category "administrative," for prisoners considered extremely dangerous, violent, or escape-prone, including people who keep attacking others inside prison. Gary's cell is roughly seven by twelve feet. A concrete bed, a concrete desk, a concrete stool, and a shower. He spends about twenty-three hours a day alone. Food comes through a slot. Exercise happens alone, in a walled pen some former prisoners have compared to an empty swimming pool. And the window is about four inches wide, angled so he can see the sky but not the landscape. Over the years, the people held there have included Zacarias Moussaoui, Dzhokhar Tsarnaev, and Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán. There is a way back: a step-down program, a slow climb through several phases that can, after years of good behaviour, lead back to an ordinary penitentiary. Gary has time to try.

[S13] OUTRO
So, the answer to the question. The level holding the most people is not the supermax and not the penitentiary. It is low security. In recent Bureau of Prisons figures it holds more people than any other level, well over a third of everyone. Most people never fall like Gary. The ladder is mostly a long, crowded middle. And one last detail. ADX Florence is part of a federal complex that also holds a camp, a medium, and a penitentiary, side by side on the same stretch of road. The whole ladder, from no fence to a four-inch window, within sight of itself. Gary just cannot see it from his. Next time, we open the file on the strangest punishments in history. I'll leave the lamp on.
