[S01] HOOK
There is a prison in Colorado where the window is about four inches wide. It is built so you can see the sky, but not where you are. Today we climb, level by level, all the way down to it.

[S02] INTRO
Good evening. I'm the Archivero. I keep the files nobody asked for. Tonight's file: every security level of the American federal prison system, from the one with no fence, to the one with no view.

[S03] LEVEL 0 — THE HALFWAY HOUSE
We start at the bottom of the ladder, which is barely a ladder. A Residential Reentry Center, better known as a halfway house. The federal Bureau of Prisons contracts them to help people near the end of a sentence rebuild a normal life. Residents go out to work, look for housing, reconnect with family, then come back at night and sign in. No cells. No bars. Just a curfew, drug tests, and a staff member who knows exactly what time you said you would be back. It is the only prison where the hardest part is the paperwork. Break the rules, though, and the ladder works in reverse. You go back up.

[S04] LEVEL 1 — MINIMUM SECURITY: THE CAMP
Next, the federal prison camp. Minimum security. Dormitory housing, a relatively low number of staff, and limited or no perimeter fencing. Yes, in some of them, you could technically walk away. People mostly don't, because walking away adds years and moves you several levels up. Camps are built around work: kitchens, groundskeeping, maintenance, sometimes jobs at the bigger prison next door. The press calls them "Club Fed." When Martha Stewart served her sentence at the camp in Alderson, West Virginia, tabloids nicknamed it "Camp Cupcake." It is not a resort. It is just the level where the walls are mostly in your head.

[S05] LEVEL 2 — LOW SECURITY
One step up: low security. Here the fence appears, and it brings a friend. Low-security federal institutions have double-fenced perimeters. Most people still live in dormitories or cubicles, rows of bunks separated by half walls, so privacy is a rumour. There are more officers per inmate than at a camp, and daily life is organised around counts, work assignments, and education programs. Many people here were convicted of non-violent crimes with longer sentences, or have a history that keeps them out of a camp. Think of it as the first level where the building is actively watching you back.

[S06] PATTERN INTERRUPT
Quick question before we go deeper. Which level do you think holds the most people? Leave your guess in the comments. The answer is less dramatic than you'd hope, which is exactly why it matters.

[S07] LEVEL 3 — MEDIUM SECURITY
Medium security. The perimeter gets stronger, often double fences with electronic detection systems, so touching the wire is not a secret for long. Housing changes too: mostly cells now, not dorms. That means a door, a bunk, a toilet, and a roommate you did not choose. Medium institutions run a wide range of work and treatment programs, but movement is more controlled. You go where you are told, when you are told, and the schedule is the real warden. Staff numbers rise again, and so do internal controls: more searches, more cameras, more counts. This is the middle of the ladder, and for many people the place where a long sentence actually happens.

[S08] LEVEL 4 — HIGH SECURITY: THE PENITENTIARY
High security. In the federal system these are the United States Penitentiaries, the USPs. Highly secured perimeters with walls or reinforced fences. Single and multiple-occupant cells. The highest ratio of staff to inmates of the regular levels, and close control of every movement: when you eat, when you walk, when you shower. This is where the system sends people convicted of the most violent crimes, or with histories of escape, gang activity, or violence inside prison. The most famous penitentiary of all is closed: Alcatraz, which operated as a federal prison from 1934 to 1963. Thirty-six men tried to escape it in fourteen attempts. Officially, none made it. Three were never found.

[S09] LEVEL 5 — THE PRISON INSIDE THE PRISON
But there is a level inside every level. Most federal institutions have a Special Housing Unit, the SHU, or as inmates call it, "the hole." This is where people go for breaking rules, during investigations, or for their own protection. Time in the SHU usually means being locked in a cell for most of the day, with meals delivered through a slot and recreation in a small cage. It is not a separate prison you are sentenced to. It is a place the prison can send you, any day, from any level. Which is why, in practice, every security level has a basement.

[S10] LEVEL 6 — ADMINISTRATIVE AND THE SUPERMAX
And now, the bottom of the file. The Bureau of Prisons calls the last category "administrative." These institutions have special missions: holding people before trial, treating serious medical cases, or containing people considered extremely dangerous, violent, or escape-prone. The most extreme is ADX Florence in Colorado, opened in 1994 and nicknamed "the Alcatraz of the Rockies." Cells are roughly seven by twelve feet [VERIFY dimensions], with a concrete bed, a concrete desk, a concrete stool, and a shower. Most prisoners spend about 23 hours a day alone in the cell. And the window is about four inches wide, angled so you see sky but not the landscape, so you cannot tell where in the building you are. People held there include Zacarias Moussaoui, Dzhokhar Tsarnaev, and Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán [VERIFY current status before publish]. At this level, the punishment is not the walls. It is the silence between them.

[S11] OUTRO
So that is the ladder: from a curfew, to a fence, to a cell, to a window four inches wide. Next time, we open the file on the strangest punishments in history. I'll leave the lamp on.
