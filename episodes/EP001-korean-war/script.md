# EP001 — The Korean War: The War That Never Ended

Channel: The Archivero · Format: Conflict summarized (geopolitics) · Target: about 22 minutes (about 3,700 words at Kokoro pace)
Status: v1, 2026-11-20. Numbers are the commonly cited estimates from the sources at the end; casualty figures vary widely between sources and are given as ranges.

---

[S01] HOOK
There is a line across the Korean Peninsula, two hundred and fifty kilometers long and four kilometers wide. On either side of it, about a million soldiers stand ready, behind minefields, fences and guard posts. Technically, the war they are guarding has never ended. There was no peace treaty. Only a ceasefire, signed in 1953, by generals who could not agree on anything except that they were tired of dying. That war killed around three million people, pulled in the United States, China and the Soviet Union, and still shapes the most dangerous border on Earth. And it started with two American officers, a map, and about thirty minutes.

[S02] INTRO
Good evening. I'm the Archivero, and I keep the files nobody asked for. Tonight's file is the Korean War, from 1950 to 1953. In the West it's often called the Forgotten War, squeezed between the Second World War and Vietnam. But almost everything about East Asia today, the two Koreas, the American bases, China's place in the world, even North Korea's nuclear weapons, traces back to these three years. So let's open the map, and go back to where the line was drawn.

[S03] A COLONY SPLIT IN TWO
For thirty-five years, from 1910 to 1945, Korea was a colony of the Empire of Japan. Koreans lost their independence, and during the Second World War hundreds of thousands were forced into labor for the Japanese war machine. In August 1945, Japan collapsed. The Soviet Union declared war on Japan on the 8th of August and its troops poured into Manchuria and northern Korea. In Washington, officials worried that the Soviets would take the whole peninsula. On the night of the 10th of August, two American officers, Colonel Charles Bonesteel and Major Dean Rusk, were given about half an hour to propose a dividing line. Using a National Geographic map, they picked the 38th parallel, because it left the capital, Seoul, on the American side. To Washington's surprise, Stalin accepted. A line meant to be temporary was now on the map.

[S04] TWO KOREAS
The temporary line hardened as the Cold War began. In the north, the Soviets backed a young communist guerrilla leader, Kim Il Sung, who had fought the Japanese in Manchuria. In the south, the Americans backed Syngman Rhee, an elderly nationalist who had spent decades in exile in the United States. Plans for elections across the whole country collapsed. In 1948 two separate states were declared: the Republic of Korea in the south in August, and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea in the north in September. Both claimed to be the only legitimate government of all Korea. Both leaders talked openly about unifying the country by force. Along the 38th parallel, border clashes killed thousands of people before the war even officially began.

[S05] THE GREEN LIGHT
Kim Il Sung needed permission and weapons, and both came from Moscow. In 1949 Stalin said no. By early 1950 the world had changed. The Soviet Union had tested its first atomic bomb. Mao Zedong's communists had won the Chinese civil war. And in January 1950, the American Secretary of State, Dean Acheson, gave a speech describing a US defense perimeter in the Pacific that did not include Korea. In the spring of 1950, Stalin finally agreed to Kim's plan, on one condition: Mao had to support it too, because if things went wrong, it would be China, not the Soviet Union, that sent troops. Mao agreed. Soviet advisers helped draw up the invasion plan. Soviet tanks, artillery and aircraft were shipped north.

[S06] JUNE 25, 1950
Before dawn on Sunday, the 25th of June 1950, the North Korean army crossed the 38th parallel along its whole length. It had around ninety thousand men in the attack and more than two hundred Soviet T-34 tanks. The South Korean army had no tanks at all, few anti-tank weapons, and many of its men were on weekend leave. The southern defenses collapsed. Three days later, Seoul fell. Bridges over the Han River were blown up while they were still packed with refugees. To many people in the West, it looked like the first move of a world war: communism on the march, testing whether anyone would stop it.

[S07] THE UNITED NATIONS GOES TO WAR
President Harry Truman decided within days that the United States would fight. And because of a strange diplomatic accident, it would not fight alone. The Soviet Union was boycotting the United Nations Security Council that year, in protest that Nationalist China, not Mao's China, held China's seat. With the Soviets absent and unable to use their veto, the Security Council condemned the invasion and called on members to help South Korea repel it. In the end, sixteen countries sent combat troops under a United Nations Command led by the United States, including Britain, Turkey, Canada, Australia, France, the Philippines, Thailand, Ethiopia and Colombia. Truman also ordered the US Seventh Fleet into the Taiwan Strait, a decision that would shape relations with China for the rest of the century.

[S08] THE PUSAN PERIMETER
The first American troops thrown into Korea came from the soft occupation army in Japan. On the 5th of July, a small unit called Task Force Smith met North Korean tanks near Osan, and was overrun. Through July, the Americans and South Koreans retreated south, fighting and losing town after town. By August, they were squeezed into a corner in the southeast of the peninsula, around the port of Pusan, today's Busan, behind a defensive line about two hundred and thirty kilometers long. This was the Pusan Perimeter. For six weeks, the North Koreans attacked it again and again. The line held, barely, as reinforcements and supplies poured in through the port. North Korea's supply lines, meanwhile, were stretched to breaking point and pounded from the air.

[S09] INCHON
Then came the most daring move of the war. General Douglas MacArthur, the commander of the UN forces, planned an amphibious landing far behind enemy lines at Inchon, the port near Seoul. Almost everyone told him it was a terrible idea. Inchon had some of the most extreme tides in the world, with only a few hours a day when ships could reach the shore, narrow channels, and sea walls instead of beaches. MacArthur argued that because it was impossible, the enemy would not expect it. On the 15th of September 1950, US Marines landed at Inchon. It worked. Within two weeks, Seoul was retaken, and the North Korean army, cut off from its supply lines, collapsed and fled north.

[S10] THE DECISION TO CROSS
Now the war's goal changed. The UN had gone to war to push the North back behind the 38th parallel. But with the enemy in ruins, Washington and MacArthur saw a chance to destroy North Korea entirely and unify the peninsula under the South. In early October, South Korean and then UN forces crossed the line. On the 19th of October, they captured Pyongyang, North Korea's capital. They pushed on towards the Yalu River, the border with China. Through the Indian ambassador in Beijing, China's premier Zhou Enlai had warned that if American troops crossed the 38th parallel, China would intervene. In Washington, the warning was treated as a bluff. MacArthur assured Truman that if the Chinese came in, there would be "the greatest slaughter."

[S11] CHINA ENTERS
It was not a bluff. On the night of the 19th of October 1950, the same day Pyongyang fell, the first units of the Chinese People's Volunteer Army began crossing the Yalu in secret, marching at night and hiding by day. Within weeks, around two hundred thousand Chinese soldiers were in North Korea, and more kept coming. They first struck in late October, then disappeared, and UN commanders misread the warning. In late November, as MacArthur launched what he hoped would be the final offensive to end the war by Christmas, the Chinese attacked in force. They hit at night, in the mountains, blowing bugles and whistles, in temperatures far below freezing. The UN advance broke. Among the Chinese soldiers killed in an air raid that November was Mao Anying, Mao Zedong's eldest son.

[S12] CHOSIN AND THE RETREAT
At the Chosin Reservoir in the northeast, around thirty thousand UN troops, most of them US Marines, were surrounded by about a hundred and twenty thousand Chinese soldiers. Temperatures dropped to minus thirty degrees Celsius. Weapons froze. Medical supplies froze. For two weeks the Marines fought their way out, south towards the sea, carrying their wounded with them. At the port of Hungnam in December, the US Navy evacuated more than a hundred thousand soldiers, and nearly a hundred thousand Korean civilians who did not want to stay under the North. One cargo ship, the SS Meredith Victory, built for twelve passengers, carried about fourteen thousand refugees in a single voyage. In the west, the UN armies fell back across the 38th parallel. In January 1951, Seoul fell for the second time.

[S13] MID QUESTION
Quick question before we go on. Was crossing the 38th parallel in October 1950 the biggest mistake of the war, or was trying to unify Korea the right call that simply went wrong? Tell me in the comments. I read the best arguments in the next file.

[S14] RIDGWAY AND THE MEAT GRINDER
In December 1950, the commander of the US Eighth Army, General Walton Walker, was killed in a jeep accident. His replacement, General Matthew Ridgway, arrived to find an army that was exhausted, demoralized and expecting to be pushed into the sea. Ridgway rebuilt it. He stopped the retreat, sent his troops back out on the attack, and changed the goal: not to capture territory, but to inflict as many losses as possible on the enemy, using overwhelming artillery and air power. His soldiers called it the meat grinder. In March 1951, Seoul changed hands for the fourth and last time. By spring, the front had stabilized roughly around the 38th parallel, almost exactly where the war had started.

[S15] TRUMAN FIRES MACARTHUR
MacArthur wanted something else. He wanted to widen the war: to bomb bases inside China, blockade the Chinese coast, bring in Nationalist Chinese troops from Taiwan, and he spoke publicly about using atomic weapons. Truman feared that would start a third world war with the Soviet Union, at a time when America's real priority was Europe. When MacArthur openly challenged the president's policy in letters and statements, Truman fired him, on the 11th of April 1951. It was one of the most controversial decisions in American political history. MacArthur came home to ticker-tape parades. But the principle held: in the United States, civilians, not generals, decide how wars are fought. And the Korean War became a limited war, fought for a stalemate, not for victory.

[S16] TWO YEARS OF TALKING AND DYING
In July 1951, armistice talks began, first at Kaesong and later at the village of Panmunjom. Everyone expected them to take weeks. They took two years. The biggest argument was about prisoners of war. Tens of thousands of Chinese and North Korean prisoners did not want to go home, and the United States refused to send them back by force. The communist side refused to accept that. While the diplomats argued, the soldiers fought brutal battles for hills that often had only numbers for names, trading the same ridges back and forth, in trenches and bunkers that looked more like the First World War than the Second. A large share of the war's casualties fell during these two years of negotiations.

[S17] THE AIR WAR
Above the trenches, the war was something else. In the skies over the northwest of Korea, a stretch the American pilots called MiG Alley, American F-86 Sabres fought Soviet-built MiG-15 jet fighters, in the first large-scale jet-against-jet combat in history. Many of the MiGs were flown in secret by Soviet pilots, something both Moscow and Washington knew, and both kept quiet to avoid a direct superpower war. On the ground below, North Korea was being destroyed. The United States dropped around six hundred and thirty-five thousand tons of bombs on Korea, including over thirty thousand tons of napalm, more than it had dropped in the entire Pacific war against Japan. Almost every city in North Korea was flattened, and the bombing later targeted dams and power plants. North Korea's leaders have used that memory ever since.

[S18] THE ARMISTICE
In March 1953, Stalin died, and the new Soviet leadership wanted the war over. A compromise was found on the prisoners: those who refused to go home would be handed to a neutral commission. On the 27th of July 1953, at Panmunjom, the armistice was signed by the UN Command and by the North Korean and Chinese commanders. South Korea's president, Syngman Rhee, who still wanted to fight on and unify the country, refused to sign. The guns stopped. A demilitarized zone was created along the front line, two kilometers deep on each side. The new border ran close to the 38th parallel, slightly north of it in the east and slightly south in the west. After three years, the map was almost exactly where it started.

[S19] THE COST
The human cost was staggering. Around thirty-six thousand American soldiers were killed. South Korea lost well over a hundred thousand soldiers. Chinese military deaths are estimated anywhere from around a hundred and eighty thousand, the official Chinese figure, to more than four hundred thousand. North Korean military losses were in the hundreds of thousands. And civilians suffered most: estimates for the total number of Koreans killed, north and south, range from two to three million, out of a population of about thirty million. Millions more became refugees, and around ten million Koreans were separated from their families by the new border. Many never saw each other again.

[S20] WHAT THE WAR CHANGED
Geopolitically, the Korean War changed far more than Korea. The United States, which had been cutting its army after 1945, tripled its defense spending, turned NATO into a real military alliance, and committed itself to containing communism everywhere in Asia, a path that led directly to Vietnam. America signed a defense treaty with South Korea, and US troops are still stationed there today, around twenty-eight thousand of them. Japan's economy boomed by supplying the UN war effort, a turning point in its postwar recovery. China, which had fought the most powerful country on Earth to a standstill only one year after its revolution, gained enormous prestige, but was cut off from the West, and Taiwan, protected by the Seventh Fleet, stayed out of its reach. And the war locked in the Cold War's map of Asia for decades.

[S21] THE TWO KOREAS TODAY
The two halves of Korea then took very different roads. South Korea went from one of the poorest countries in the world to a rich, high-tech democracy, after decades of military rule along the way. North Korea became the most closed state on Earth, ruled by Kim Il Sung, then his son, then his grandson, in the world's only communist dynasty. It built nuclear weapons, arguing that only a bomb would stop an American attack like the one it remembers from the 1950s. The armistice still holds. Soldiers from both sides still stand face to face in the blue huts at Panmunjom. And every few years, a shelling, a sinking or a missile test reminds the world that the Korean War was paused, not finished.

[S22] OUTRO
So that's the file of the Korean War. A line drawn in thirty minutes, a war that swept up and down a peninsula three times in one year, two years of fighting over hills while diplomats argued about prisoners, and a ceasefire that has now lasted more than seventy years. Forgotten in the West, remembered every day in Korea. Next time, we open another file. I'll leave the lamp on.
