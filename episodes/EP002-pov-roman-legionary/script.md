# EP001 — POV: You're a Roman Legionary

Channel: The Archivero · Format: POV (second person) · Target: about 21 minutes (about 3,600 words at Kokoro pace)
Status: v1, 2026-11-20. "You" are a composite recruit; the events, rules, numbers and quotes are real (see Sources).

---

[S01] HOOK
It is September, in the year 9 AD. You are twenty-six years old, soaked to the bone, and standing in a German forest so thick you cannot see the man three places ahead of you in the column. It has rained for two days. Your sandals are full of mud, your shield is twice as heavy wet, and somewhere in the trees, thousands of men who used to be your allies are quietly moving into position. By the end of this week, your legion will not exist. Its number will never be used again. This is how you got here.

[S02] INTRO
Good evening. I'm the Archivero, and I keep the files nobody asked for. Tonight's file is a uniform. Red tunic, iron helmet, hobnailed sandals: the Roman legionary, the most famous soldier in history. For about four hundred years, men like you held an empire together that stretched from Scotland to Syria. Not with genius generals, mostly. With walking, digging, paperwork, and a level of discipline that would make a modern drill sergeant faint. So let's put the helmet on you, and see how long you last.

[S03] RECRUITMENT
Rewind eight years, to the year 1 AD. You are eighteen, the son of a poor farmer in northern Italy, and you have a problem: your older brother gets the farm, and you get nothing. A century earlier that would have kept you out of the army entirely, because Roman soldiers were supposed to own property. But after the reforms traditionally credited to the general Gaius Marius, the legions started taking the poor. Now the army is a career. Steady pay, a roof, three meals a day, and a big bonus at the end, if you survive. You walk to the recruiting officer with a letter of recommendation from a local big man, because yes, even in ancient Rome, it is who you know. You must be a Roman citizen, healthy, and tall enough. A doctor checks your eyes, your teeth, your limbs. You pass. They give you a small lead tag to wear around your neck, a kind of ancient dog tag, and you swear the military oath, the sacramentum: to obey your commander, to never desert, and to never abandon the standards. Congratulations. You belong to the state now.

[S04] SERVICE TERMS
Read the contract you did not get to read. Under the emperor Augustus, a legionary signed up for sixteen years, plus four more in reserve. In the year 5 AD that became twenty years, plus five more as a veteran on call. Later it would simply be twenty-five. You may not marry while you serve. If you already have a wife, as far as the law is concerned, you don't. Your pay is two hundred and twenty-five silver denarii a year. That sounds fine until the deductions arrive: food, boots, clothing, your tent, the burial club that will pay for your funeral, and a forced savings account. Some soldiers finished the year with almost nothing in their hands. And no, you were not paid in salt. The idea that "salary" comes from Roman soldiers being paid in salt is mostly a myth. You were paid in coins. Small ones.

[S05] TRAINING
Now you learn to be a Roman. According to the Roman writer Vegetius, recruits had to march twenty Roman miles, around eighteen of ours, in five summer hours at the regular military pace, and twenty-four at the fast pace. While carrying a load of about sixty Roman pounds. You train twice a day. You fight a wooden post with a wooden sword and a wicker shield, both made twice as heavy as the real thing, so the real ones feel light. You learn to stab, never slash, because a stab kills and a slash just annoys people. You learn to throw a javelin, to swim, to jump, and above all, to dig. Because the secret weapon of the Roman army is not the sword. It is the shovel. Every single night on campaign, the entire legion builds a fortified camp from scratch: a ditch, an earth rampart, a palisade of stakes, and neat streets of tents in the same layout every time, so that every man knows exactly where his bed is, in the dark, in any country on earth.

[S06] KIT
Let's dress you. A wool tunic, a cloak, and caligae: heavy sandals with iron hobnails in the soles, which grip the road and announce you from a mile away. A helmet with big cheek guards. Body armour: a shirt of iron mail, or the famous plate armour made of overlapping iron strips. A curved rectangular shield, the scutum, about as tall as your chest. Two javelins, the pila, with long soft iron necks designed to bend when they hit, so the enemy cannot throw them back, and a shield with a bent javelin stuck in it becomes useless. A dagger. And the gladius, a short sword built for stabbing at close range. Then the luggage. On campaign you carry a pole over your shoulder loaded with a cooking pot, a saw, a pickaxe, a basket for moving earth, a rope, a sickle, and several days of food. The Jewish historian Josephus, who watched Roman legions up close, listed almost exactly that. Soldiers carried so much that they got a nickname: Marius's mules.

[S07] YOUR UNIT
You are not alone. You are one of eight men in a contubernium: a tent group. Eight men, one leather tent, one mule, one cooking fire. These seven strangers are now your family, your kitchen, and the people who will be ordered to punish you if you fail them. Ten tent groups make a century: eighty men, commanded by a centurion. Six centuries make a cohort. Ten cohorts make a legion, around five thousand men, with the first cohort at double strength. At the top of the legion stands its eagle, the aquila, a golden eagle on a pole, carried into battle. The eagle is the soul of the legion. Losing it is the worst disgrace a Roman unit can suffer. Remember that, because it matters later. Your legion is the Eighteenth.

[S08] A DAY ON THE MARCH
On campaign, every day starts before sunrise with three trumpet calls. At the first, you take down the tent. At the second, you load the mule and your own pole. At the third, the column moves out, and woe to anyone still packing. The order of march is always the same: scouts and light troops in front, then the surveyors and engineers who will mark out tonight's camp, then the general's baggage, the cavalry, the siege engines, the legions in long columns six men wide, and the camp followers at the back. You march for five or six hours, singing rude songs about your own generals, which Roman soldiers were famous for. In the early afternoon you reach the spot the surveyors have chosen, and instead of resting, you put down your load and start digging the camp. By evening the legion is inside a fortress that did not exist that morning. You eat, you stand guard in shifts through the night, and tomorrow you burn it, or leave it, and do the whole thing again.

[S09] THE EMPIRE YOU GUARD
Look at a map of your new world. Under Augustus there are about twenty-eight legions, around a hundred and fifty thousand citizen soldiers, plus roughly as many again in the auxiliary units made up of non-citizens from the provinces. That sounds like a lot until you see the size of the empire. Almost none of these men are in Italy. They sit on the edges: in Spain, in the Balkans, in Syria, in Egypt, and above all along the great rivers in the north, the Rhine and the Danube. Beyond the Rhine lies Germania, a land of forests, marshes and dozens of tribes, which Augustus has decided to turn into a province. Your legion, the Eighteenth, is stationed on the Rhine frontier. Your job, for the next twenty years, is to be the wall. Not a stone wall. A wall made of men who are always there, always watching, always ready to march. The Romans would build actual stone walls later, like Hadrian's Wall in Britain. For now, the wall is you.

[S10] DAILY LIFE
Most of a legionary's life is not battle. It is chores. In winter quarters or in a permanent fort you do guard duty, cleaning, road building, bridge building, quarrying stone, cutting firewood, and paperwork, because the Roman army ran on paperwork. Letters found at the fort of Vindolanda, near Hadrian's Wall, written about a century after your time, show what soldiers worried about. One man was sent socks, two pairs of sandals, and two pairs of underpants. Another letter is a birthday party invitation between two officers' wives. You eat a ration of wheat, which you grind and bake yourself, plus whatever your tent group can add: bacon, cheese, lentils, vegetables, and posca, sour wine mixed with water. On the march you eat hard, dry biscuit. You gamble with dice. You go to the bath house, because a proper Roman fort has one. And you complain. Every army in history complains. Yours does too.

[S11] PROMOTION
Not everyone stays at the bottom. If you can read, write, count, or do anything useful, you might become an immunis: a specialist excused from the worst chores. Surveyors, engineers, clerks, medics, blacksmiths, butchers, even the men who look after the weapons. Above them are the principales, junior officers on pay and a half or double pay. The optio is the centurion's deputy. The tesserarius runs the guard and hands out the nightly password, written on a little tablet. The signifer carries the century's standard and also looks after the men's savings, which makes him a mix between a flag bearer and a bank manager. The aquilifer carries the legion's eagle itself, the most honoured and most dangerous job in the unit. And above them all, the centurions, paid many times more than you, with their crests worn sideways across the helmet so their men can find them in the chaos. A few ordinary soldiers really did rise to centurion after many years. Most did not. You, for now, are scrubbing the latrines.

[S12] MONEY AND FUN
Outside the walls of every big fortress grows a second town, the canabae: taverns, shops, craftsmen, merchants, and families. Because soldiers cannot legally marry, thousands of them simply live with partners there anyway, and have children the law pretends not to see. This is where your pay goes. Wine, better food, dice, a new cloak, a good-luck charm, a letter written for you by a scribe if you cannot write. Soldiers saved money, lent it to each other, and lost it gambling. Some had slaves of their own. And all of them listened for news of a donative: an extra cash gift from the emperor, usually at the start of a new reign or after a victory, which could be worth months of pay. Life in a Roman fort was hard and boring, but it was also oddly comfortable for the time. Regular food, a doctor, a bath house and a salary were more than most farmers in the empire would ever have.

[S13] GODS AND STANDARDS
The army is also a church. In the middle of every fortress stands the headquarters building, and in its heart is a shrine holding the legion's eagle and the standards, guarded day and night. Beneath the shrine, very conveniently, sits the strongroom with the pay chest, so if you want to rob the army, you have to rob the gods first. Every year you renew your oath to the emperor. The army keeps a calendar of festivals for the emperor's family, for Mars, for Jupiter, for the birthday of the eagle itself. Soldiers also bring their own gods. A century or two later, many legionaries will worship Mithras, a god of light whose followers meet in small dark underground temples, with secret ranks like a club. You touch your lucky charm, you pour a little wine on the ground for the gods, and you hope they are paying attention. In a few years you will find out they were not.

[S14] MID QUESTION
Quick question before your legion marches into Germany. Out of everything so far, the twenty years, the marching, the digging, the no marriage, the underpants letters, what would make you quit first? Tell me in the comments. I'll read the worst answers in the next file.

[S15] DISCIPLINE
You will not quit, because quitting is desertion, and Roman discipline is terrifying. Your centurion carries a vine stick, the vitis, the symbol of his rank, and he uses it on your back. The historian Tacitus describes a centurion called Lucilius, nicknamed by his men "Bring me another", because he broke his stick over so many soldiers that he kept shouting for a new one. When his men mutinied, they killed him. Fall asleep on night guard, and you may face the fustuarium: your own comrades beat or stone you to death. And if a whole unit runs away in battle, the commander can order a decimation: the unit is divided into groups of ten, lots are drawn, and in each group the other nine kill the one who lost. It was rare, but famous. In 71 BC, during the war against Spartacus, Marcus Crassus revived it. Nobody in your century plans to be the one in ten.

[S16] WOUNDS AND MEDICINE
Before your first battle, the good news: the Roman army has some of the best medicine in the ancient world. Big forts have a hospital, the valetudinarium, with wards of small rooms around a courtyard. There are doctors, and orderlies who carry bandages onto the battlefield. Archaeologists have found Roman scalpels, forceps, probes, bone levers and needles, many of them surprisingly similar to modern tools. Surgeons could pull out arrowheads, stitch cuts, set broken bones, and amputate. The bad news: there are no antibiotics, no anaesthesia beyond strong wine and herbs, and nobody knows what germs are. A clean stab wound might heal. A dirty one in your belly almost certainly kills you, slowly. So most soldiers died not from a famous battle, but from fevers, dysentery, infected wounds and accidents. The enemy you should fear most is not a barbarian with an axe. It is the water in your camp.

[S17] FIRST BATTLE
Your first real fight comes on a frontier river, against a raiding party. It is nothing like the stories. The noise comes first: horns, shouting, the drumming of weapons on shields. Then the order. You throw your first javelin, then your second, into the mass of men running at you. The iron necks bend, shields drop, and then the lines hit. You do not swing your sword like a hero in a painting. You crouch behind your shield, punch forward with its iron boss, and stab low, under the other man's shield, over and over, while the man behind you pushes, and the man beside you covers your right side. A Roman battle line is a machine made of tired, terrified people trusting each other. When the enemy breaks and runs, you are too exhausted to cheer. That night you dig a camp. Of course you do.

[S18] TESTUDO AND SIEGES
The Roman army had another face, the one you see in films. The testudo, the tortoise: a block of men locking their shields together on every side and over their heads, so they can walk right up to an enemy wall under a rain of arrows and stones. The siege engines: the ballista, a giant crossbow that throws stones, and the scorpio, a smaller bolt thrower so accurate that Roman writers boast about it pinning enemies to the ground. And the most Roman thing of all: when Julius Caesar besieged the Gallic leader Vercingetorix at Alesia, half a century before your time, he did not storm the town. He built a ring of walls, ditches and towers all the way around it, and then a second ring facing outwards, against the relief army coming to rescue it. His men fought both armies at once, from inside their own fortifications, and won. Your instructors tell that story a lot. It mainly means more digging.

[S19] INTO GERMANY
In the spring of 9 AD your three legions cross the Rhine and march deep into Germania for the summer. Your general, Publius Quinctilius Varus, behaves as if the province is already conquered. He holds courts, hands down Roman judgments, and collects taxes from tribes who are not used to paying anyone. You spend the summer near the river Weser, building, patrolling and being bored. The local Germanic leaders smile, trade and dine with Varus. The most trusted of all is Arminius, a prince of the Cherusci tribe, who grew up in Rome, served as an officer in the Roman army and holds Roman citizenship. Another German noble, Segestes, warns Varus that Arminius is planning a revolt. Varus thinks it is a family feud and ignores him. At the end of the summer, the army starts back towards its winter camps on the Rhine.

[S20] THE WORST DAY: TEUTOBURG
And now we're back in the rain. On the way home, news arrives of an uprising among a distant tribe, and Arminius suggests the army make a short detour to crush it. Varus agrees. Arminius rides ahead, saying he will gather his warriors to help. He does gather them. Just not to help. The detour leads your column, three legions, auxiliaries, cavalry, wagons, mules, servants, women and children, stretched out for miles, into a strip of land squeezed between wooded hills and a great bog, near what the Romans would remember as the Teutoburg Forest. Then the trees start throwing spears. For three or four days the column is attacked from cover, in mud and rain, in terrain where you cannot form a battle line, cannot build a proper camp, and cannot see who is killing you. The tribes Arminius has secretly united pick the army apart, piece by piece. Varus, wounded, falls on his own sword rather than be captured. Almost the entire force is destroyed, somewhere around fifteen to twenty thousand people. The eagles are taken. Two thousand years later, archaeologists at a place called Kalkriese, near the modern city of Osnabrück, found thousands of Roman objects in the ground: coins, weapons, armour, hobnails from sandals like yours, and the iron face mask of a Roman cavalryman. One of your officers, a centurion named Marcus Caelius, has a tombstone that still survives in a museum. It says he fell in the war of Varus, and that his bones may one day be placed in the tomb. They never were.

[S21] AFTERMATH
In Rome, the old emperor Augustus takes the news so badly that, according to the historian Suetonius, he lets his hair and beard grow for months and bangs his head against a door, shouting: "Quinctilius Varus, give me back my legions!" The numbers Seventeen, Eighteen and Nineteen are never used for a Roman legion again. Six years later, a Roman army under Germanicus reaches the battlefield and finds the bones still lying there, skulls nailed to trees, and buries what it can. Two of the lost eagles are recovered in the following years, and the last one only in 41 AD, more than thirty years after it was taken. Rome never conquers Germany beyond the Rhine. A single ambush in the rain helps draw a border that, in a way, Europe still feels today: the line between the Latin world and the Germanic one.

[S22] THE OTHER ENDINGS
But let's be generous. Say you were transferred to another legion a year before. What happens to you? If disease, accidents or battle don't get you, and many soldiers did die long before their discharge, you serve your twenty years plus your reserve years. Then comes the honourable discharge, the honesta missio. Under Augustus a retiring legionary received a bonus of about three thousand denarii, more than thirteen years of basic pay, or a plot of land in a veterans' colony. You would be in your mid-forties, with a bad back, iron-hard feet, and a family the law finally recognises. Many veterans settled near the forts where they served, married local women, and their sons joined the same army. That is how Rome turned frontier camps into cities: places like Cologne, York, and many others started out, in one way or another, as Roman military sites.

[S23] OUTRO
So that's your file. Twenty-five years of marching, digging, chores, terrifying discipline and the occasional nightmare in a forest, for a pension and a piece of land. Most legionaries never saw a famous battle. They built roads and walls that are still standing two thousand years later, which is more than most of us will be able to say about our jobs. Next time, we open another uniform. I'll leave the lamp on.
